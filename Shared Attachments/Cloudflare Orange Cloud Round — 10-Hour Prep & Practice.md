## The syllabus: what is assessed

There is no official question list. The official syllabus is Cloudflare's six Capabilities, which the company says it uses when measuring success, giving feedback and rewarding performance ([Cloudflare Careers](https://www.cloudflare.com/careers?gh_src=GradIRL)). Its formula: Performance = Results + Behaviors. This round tests the Behaviors half.

| Capability (official name) | What it means | What a strong answer shows | Your best evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Be curious to learn and grow | Approach challenges eagerly, ask questions, look for ways to improve | Something you dug into unprompted, learned, then applied or taught | Scala/Akka ramp-up; Spark 3→4 row-count investigation; MCP Toolbox work |
| Communicate clearly and transparently | Share information openly, give direct feedback, keep people informed | Bad news raised early; message changed for the audience | Explaining CREST to non-engineers; incident updates |
| Do the right thing | Act with integrity, make principled decisions, own your actions | The right call over the easy one; a mistake owned out loud | S5 mistake story; refusing a shortcut |
| Embrace diversity | Value different perspectives and experiences | You changed your mind because a different view was right | S4 conflict story; ShivAgri users who are not technical |
| Get your work across the finish line | Own commitments, follow through, deliver results | Pushed to done through blockers, with a measured result | CREST: 3+ days to under 4 hours |
| Lead with empathy | Assume good intent, support teammates, keep things welcoming | Helped someone, or designed around a user's real situation | ShivAgri for farmers in Gujarat; S7 feedback or mentoring |

The same page describes the culture as **Principled, Curious, Transparent**. Use those words naturally once or twice, never as a checklist.

**What a sales interviewer is likely to probe**

- Can you explain your work without jargon? Expect a version of "explain that to me like I'm a customer."
- Do you connect engineering choices to customer impact? Outages land on his accounts first.
- How do you work with non-engineers: PMs, business, operations?
- Would he want you on a customer escalation call: calm, honest, owns the problem?

**Card format for every story** (90–120 seconds)

- **Headline:** one sentence with the result in it.
- **Situation and task:** two sentences. Who, what was at stake.
- **Actions:** three "I" verbs. Not "we."
- **Result:** one number plus what changed for people.
- **Limitation:** what you'd do differently. This line is what makes it sound real.
- **Capability hook:** the closing sentence that names the behavior without naming the Capability.

### S1 — CREST orchestration rebuild

- **Headline:** I rebuilt the system that coordinates our regulatory stress tests, taking a run from more than 3 days to under 4 hours.
- **Plain-English setup for Kunal:** Banks have to prove to regulators they would survive a crisis. We run thousands of "what if markets crash" simulations. When a run takes 3 days, a mistake found on Friday is fixed next Wednesday.
- **Actions:** fill in your three. Candidates from your resume: the orchestration engine design, parallelising the work across services, checkpointing so failures don't restart everything.
- **Result:** 3+ days to under 4 hours. Your resume also lists 500 GB+ across 100+ services, 40% throughput, 99.9% uptime and 60% lower MTTR. Use only the numbers you can defend line by line.
- **Limitation (fill in):** what broke or what you'd design differently.
- **Watch out:** be exact about what was inherited and what you built. This was flagged in your Google prep and it matters more with a non-engineer, who will judge you on candour, not architecture.

### S2 — An investigation you chased

Pick one, whichever has the clearest stakes:

- Spark 3 to Spark 4 join returning different row counts; your hypothesis was CHAR/VARCHAR padding.
- Terraform Enterprise failures traced to Redis private service access IP range exhaustion.
- Binary Authorization policies blocking image deployments on GCP.

Fill in: who noticed, what would have happened if nobody chased it, the moment you found the cause, who you told and how, what you changed so it can't recur. This story answers "investigated because it bothered you" and "decision with incomplete information."

### S3 — ShivAgri: building for farmers

- **Headline:** On my own time I built and still run shivagri.com, a farm-management platform for farmers in Gujarat who are not technical users.
- **Facts you have:** solo full-stack build on Node.js, Angular and MongoDB; role-based access, soil and fertilizer testing modules, PDF reports, media management; tracked in Linear.
- **Fill in:** one real moment where a user's situation changed your design (language, low-end phones, printed PDF reports, connectivity). That moment is the empathy evidence. Without it, this is only a tech list.
- **Why it lands with Kunal:** it shows you build for the person on the other end, which is his whole job.

### S4 — Conflict with a different perspective (scaffold)

- [ ] Who (role, not name), what you disagreed about, and why their view was reasonable from where they sat.
- [ ] What you did: asked questions, gathered data, proposed a test or compromise.
- [ ] Outcome, and specifically what you learned from their side. Best version: you changed your mind on at least part of it.

* Avoid stories where you were simply right and they came round. That scores as "won an argument," not "embrace diversity."

### S5 — A mistake you owned (scaffold)

- [ ] What you did wrong, in one plain sentence. No passive voice.
- [ ] Who was affected and how soon you told them.
- [ ] How you fixed it, and the change that stops it happening again (a check, a test, a rollout step).
- [ ] What you do differently today.

* Tie-in if true: Cloudflare's own Nov 2025 outage came from a configuration rollout. If your mistake involved config, a deploy or skipped validation, say how you'd now roll it out gradually. It shows you read their postmortem without saying so.

## Practice drills

Practise out loud and record on your phone. Reading stories silently does not transfer to a live call. The interview's AI ban covers Monday's call, not your prep.

### Drill A — Rapid-fire headlines (20 min)

For each prompt, say the story ID and its one-line headline within 15 seconds. Go round twice, shuffled. The goal is recall speed, not polish.

1. A mistake you made
2. Something you learned and shared
3. A conflict with someone who saw it differently
4. Something you're proud of
5. Something you learned outside work
6. A decision with incomplete information
7. A time you went beyond your role to finish something
8. A time you put a user's needs first
9. Tough feedback you received
10. Something you investigated because it bothered you
11. A time you chose the right call over the easy one
12. A time you explained something technical to a non-technical person

### Drill B — Explain it to sales (20 min)

Sixty seconds each. Rules: no acronyms, one analogy, one number. Best test: explain to someone at home who isn't an engineer and ask them to repeat it back.

1. What CREST does and why the bank cares.
2. Why going from 3 days to 4 hours matters to the business, not the engineers.
3. What Kubernetes does, in one analogy.
4. What Cloudflare does (the "grandmother" question).
5. What went wrong in Cloudflare's November 2025 outage and what they changed, in two sentences.
6. What the team you'd join builds, from the job description.

### Drill C — Follow-up ladder (15 min)

Run every story through these five probes. A story that can't survive them gets swapped out.

1. What exactly did you do, versus the team?
2. What would you do differently now?
3. Who was unhappy, and what did it cost?
4. How do you know it worked?
5. What did you learn that you still use?

### Self-scoring rubric

Score each answer straight after the mock. Target 10 out of 12; rewrite anything under 8.

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Specific | General habits ("I usually…") | One event, thin detail | One event with a date, person and number |
| Ownership | Only "we" | Mixed | Clear "I" actions, credit shared honestly |
| Result | None | Vague outcome | Measured outcome plus who it helped |
| Candour | No downside | Downside mentioned in passing | Real limitation or lesson, stated plainly |
| Plain English | Jargon a salesperson couldn't follow | One or two unexplained terms | A non-engineer could repeat it back |
| Length | Over 3 min or under 45 s | 2–3 min | 90–120 s, then stops |

**What Code Orange changed** (worth knowing because it is your future team's territory)

- Config changes now roll out progressively, with live health checks and automatic rollback, the way software releases already did.
- Systems fall back to the last known good config. Otherwise they fail open or closed, case by case.
- Services are split by customer cohort; Workers, for example, rolls out to free customers first.
- Backup access paths now exist for 18 key services.
- A mandatory engineering rulebook, the Codex, is enforced through AI code review.
- Customer comms: alert as soon as an issue is confirmed, update every 30–60 minutes during incidents, publish a detailed postmortem after. This is the part Kunal lived through.
